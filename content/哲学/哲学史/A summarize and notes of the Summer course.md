#### threefold internalstructure of truth

The meaning of a declarative sentence is its truth value. If we agree with Frege's view and accept fallibility, then we will face the issue of "The requirement of truth." In Professor Koch's view, all "truth theories" have more or less incorporated the three aspects of truth mentioned above. Before Professor Koch further criticizes these three aspects of truth, he asks us to first understand the difference between "Sein" and "Schein" in German, which were discussed in previous lectures. "Schein" in German has both the meaning of "appearance" and the sense of evidence and light. Something that is a non-appearance (Schein) is the "Thing in itself," an entity that is completely independent of other things and our opinions about it, and it is "epistemically inaccessible." On the other hand, appearance is what "shows" and presents to us in perception as a "phenomenon," and it is "epistemically accessible."

  

After understanding these concepts, Professor Koch believes that for one of the three aspects of truth, if one aspect is unilaterally emphasized and the other aspects of truth are ignored, it will lead to bias and contradiction. I will explain why Professor Koch thinks this way next.

The **"Realism aspect of truth"** (introduction, and the problems that can arise from unilaterally emphasizing this aspect)  
In metaphysics, there is a concept known as metaphysical realism, which posits that truth requires a correspondence between appearance and the "Thing in itself." However, appearance clearly cannot correspond to the "Thing in itself," as the latter is "epistemically inaccessible."

Semantic Realism differs from metaphysical realism in that it can choose not to acknowledge "the correspondence between appearance and the Thing in itself."

Semantic realism holds that 1) Propositional reference is identified with its truth conditions. The theory adheres to the principle of Bivalence, and 2) There are no "Truth-Value Gaps." In contrast, there is "Anti-Realism," which posits that "Propositional reference is the conditions under which it can be asserted, meaning it is the sum of all conditions under which members of the relevant linguistic community can accept the assertion of this proposition."

**Presentational Aspect of Truth** (or Phenomenal Aspect of Truth) This theory of truth posits that truth is the appearance that is given to us. If truth can be divided into the aspects of sensibility and rationality, then sensibility corresponds to "passive receptivity," which is presentational and phenomenal, while "rationality" corresponds to "activity," which is the Realism aspect. However, in reality, we cannot recognize truth by isolating either of these aspects. Furthermore, Sellars criticized "the myth of the given," which suggests that truth is merely passive perception or sense-data, and understanding truth one-sidedly as the appearance of things is an untenable theory. **Pragmatic Aspect of Truth** This theory of truth holds that "Propositional reference is the conditions under which it can be asserted." It denies 1) the principle of Bivalence and believes 2) that there are Truth-Value Gaps. The idea that we cannot know the truth or falsity of certain propositions involving the future or modalities is unacceptable.

### L 5

#### a) Objects and predications: Logical picturing

Logical picturing is a method where **"Theaetetus"** (bold print) represents "Theaetetus sings."  
By using bold print to express "Theaetetus sings," the advantage is that it eliminates the need for predicates to describe a subject. It simply uses a picturing method to convey a fact without relying on the binary nature of predicates to determine whether the "situation" actually exists, thus becoming a "fact." However, the disadvantage is that it is not possible to find a pictorial representation for all facts. Whether using a "language of singing" or different colors or fonts, these methods cannot cover the full range of our discussions. Therefore, using predicates to describe the subject is clearer compared to "pictorial language" in this regard.

#### b) Categories of objects：qualitative identity and numerical identity

Here, I introduce Black's two-sphere universe to illustrate that qualitative identity cannot guarantee numerical identity. (If we do not presuppose indexical properties.)

This thought experiment posits a universe with two iron spheres at the center, which are perfectly symmetrical in nature on the left and right, excluding numerical properties and difference properties. The term "nature" here does not include numerical properties and difference properties because difference properties are not the most primitive properties. For instance, the left and right iron spheres are different simply because they are different, a property that presupposes other more primitive properties, such as density and color. Therefore, asserting that they have difference properties is based on these more fundamental properties.

In Professor Koch's view, unless we presuppose indexical properties, which in turn presuppose that a body exists in a specific spatiotemporal region, we would face the conundrum of not being able to distinguish between the two iron spheres. That is, they possess qualitative identity but cannot guarantee numerical identity.
In fact, according to Professor Koch's conception, when a body exists within four-dimensional spacetime, in this example, we can refer to the left iron sphere as having the indexical property of "hereness" and the right iron sphere as having the indexical property of "thereness." Thus, the qualitative properties of the two spheres are not the same, which allows us to distinguish them as two numerically different iron spheres. We can also predict that in this case, qualitative identity guarantees numerical identity. #### Time theory
![[Pasted image 20240720203329.png]]
The time theory proposed by John M.E. McTaggart (1866–1925) is pioneering and has profound insights, opening up a new thinking path for the research of time philosophy. McTaggart distinguished three series of events and corresponding temporal scales. The first is the A-series, which clearly divides time into the past, the present, and the future. This division highlights the dynamics and subjectivity of time, as past events have become memories, future events have not yet arrived, and the present is a constantly flowing and changing moment. The B-series describes the sequence of events with "earlier" and "later". Although the B-series seems to be a completely objective temporal expression, McTaggart believes that the arrow direction of the B-series actually stems from the subjective A-series. This means that the objectivity presented by the B-series is not pure and absolute. The C-series is relatively complex and ambiguous. Currently, the exact meaning of the C-series as defined by McTaggart is not entirely clear. As a Hegel scholar, he might have conceived of a logical continuity behind time, which might not have a temporal arrow but a logical sequence. For the sake of simplicity, the C-series is interpreted as conforming to the description of time in fundamental physics, that is, there is no arrow and no internal asymmetry. The only difference from a spatial straight line lies in its symmetrical nomological predetermination. McTaggart's theory has sparked widespread discussion and reflection. In contemporary time philosophy, many philosophers hold different views on his theory. Contemporary philosophers called "B-theorists" regard the B-series (and B-time) as the basis, consider the C-series (and C-time) as a special case of quantum mechanics, and treat the A-series (and A-time) as our subjective fiction. McTaggart's time theory challenges our traditional cognition of the essence of time. It prompts us to deeply think about the relationship between the subjectivity and objectivity of time, as well as the meaning and value of different time scales and series. His theory not only has had a profound impact in the field of philosophy but also provides important ideological resources for the research of time in related disciplines such as physics and psychology. Although McTaggart's theory may have some controversies and unclear points, it undoubtedly has stimulated the continuous exploration and innovative thinking of the complex concept of time in the academic circle, promoting the continuous development and improvement of the time theory.

#### My review

Among the three time theories proposed by John M.E. McTaggart, in terms of daily life, the time theory of the A-series is to some extent more in line with our common sense cognition. The A-series clearly divides time into the past, the present, and the future, and this division is closely linked to our perception and experience in daily life. We can clearly feel that past events have become memories and cannot be changed; the future is full of uncertainties and possibilities and has not yet arrived; while the present is the moment we are experiencing and grasping. For example, when we plan a trip, we will consider the future vacation arrangement; when recalling the good experiences in the past, we will lament the passage of time; and at present, we will focus on preparing various matters for the trip. This clear distinction between the past, the present, and the future guides our decisions and actions. In contrast, the B-series simply describes the sequence of events with "earlier" and "later". Although it is objectively logical to some extent, its explanation for our perception and emotional experience in daily life is relatively weak. Due to its relatively complex and ambiguous definition, the C-series is not easily understood and applied intuitively in daily applications. However, this does not mean that the time theory of the A-series is perfect. It may overly emphasize the subjectivity and dynamics of time and appear less accurate in some scenarios that require precise measurement and objective description of time. But in terms of common sense perception in daily life, the A-series can better reflect our natural feelings about time, help us understand and plan various affairs in life, and handle emotions and memories related to time. Therefore, on the whole, the time theory of the A-series is more in line with common sense in daily life.

**"Exploration on Happiness, Justice and Related Issues"**

In the contemplation of happiness, we discover that it has three key aspects, corresponding respectively to the aspects of truth and the modes of time, namely pleasure, contemplation and success. Based on this, Aristotle distinguished three forms of life: the consumptive life of pleasure-seeking, the contemplative life focused on theory and the practical life involving politics.

Aristotle's exposition of these three forms of life in "Nicomachean Ethics" provides us with a profound perspective for understanding the multi-dimensional nature of happiness. The consumptive life focuses on obtaining happiness through material and sensory enjoyment, and this form of life is often associated with immediate satisfaction and external material consumption. However, relying solely on material and sensory pleasures is short-lived and unstable, and it may not bring lasting happiness.

The contemplative life places emphasis on the pursuit of truth and knowledge, achieving inner satisfaction through in-depth thinking and rational exploration. This form of life pays more attention to spiritual growth and the accumulation of wisdom. However, in real life, completely immersing oneself in contemplation and neglecting practice may lead to disconnection from society.

Political participation in practical life provides individuals with the opportunity to realize their own value and contribute to the public good through action and social interaction. Nevertheless, political life is also fraught with complexity and uncertainty. Power struggles and conflicts of interest may pose numerous challenges to individuals in the pursuit of justice and the public good.

Although each of these three forms of life has its unique value, integrating them into a unified form of life is extremely difficult. In real life, people often sway between different forms of life and find it hard to strike a perfect balance. Problems such as natural disasters, technical accidents and moral injustice frequently plague humanity, setting numerous obstacles on the path of people's pursuit of happiness. These unfortunate events not only destroy people's material lives but also bring pain and confusion at the spiritual level.

Facing the hardships and injustices in life, Kant proposed the so-called postulates of pure reason in "Critique of Practical Reason", particularly the immortality of the soul and the existence of God. Kant's viewpoint attempts to provide people with spiritual consolation and moral guidance when facing life's predicaments. He believes that the immortality of the soul gives people the hope of continuous progress on the long road of moral cultivation, and the existence of God symbolizes ultimate justice and fairness, providing a powerful impetus for people to adhere to moral principles.

However, Kant's these postulates have also sparked widespread discussion and controversy. Some people think that these postulates lack empirical basis and are merely a construction based on faith and assumption. In an era that advocates science and rationality, relying on supernatural existence to explain and solve moral problems seems somewhat powerless. But on the other hand, we cannot completely deny the positive role of these postulates on the spiritual level for humanity. When facing inexplicable and unchangeable hardships, faith and hope can give people inner strength and help them maintain their yearning and pursuit of a better life.

The exploration of issues such as happiness and suffering, justice and injustice is not only the realm of philosophical thinking but also a practical issue closely related to each of our lives. **Comparative Overview of Philosophical Approaches** In this comprehensive review, we delve into the vast landscape of philosophical thought, contrasting classical and early modern perspectives with those of contemporary analytic and hermeneutic philosophy. We also outline the speaker's systematic conception of philosophy, developed throughout the lectures. **Classical Philosophy: Plato and Aristotle** Plato's philosophy is encapsulated in his dialogues and the oral teachings reported by Aristotle, known as ta dógmata ágrapha. Plato envisioned philosophy as the doctrine of being, with the Forms or Ideas representing the true reality, with the most general or highest genera being the most real. His ontology is intertwined with epistemology; the physical world is perceived through the senses as only half real, while the forms are grasped through pre-discursive thinking. Philosophers, according to Plato, strive to fully understand these forms and articulate them through dialectics, creating logical images of true existence. For Plato, the idea of the good stands supreme, followed by the indeterminate duality, and his philosophy is not merely ontological but also ethical, with justice at its core. He believed that the doctrine of Forms could prove the reality of justice, integrating ontology, ethics, and a religious perspective. Aristotle diverged from Plato, arguing that the most general is not the most real but rather a conceptual abstraction. He categorized sciences into theoretical, practical, and technical, with metaphysics, physics, and mathematics as theoretical sciences. Metaphysics, for Aristotle, deals with being as being and is the science of ontological principles, most notably the principle of non-contradiction. Philosophers like Descartes, Locke, and Hume questioned the nature of knowledge, existence, and the mind's relationship with the external world. It is concerned with the foundations of mathematics, language, and science, and how these areas inform our understanding of reality. Hermeneutic philosophy, on the other hand, is concerned with the interpretation of texts and the understanding of human experience. It emphasizes the role of context, history, and culture in shaping meaning and rejects the idea of a single, objective truth. **Systematic Philosophy** The speaker's own conception of systematic philosophy is introduced as logical archaeology, focusing on the presuppositions underlying truth claims.
