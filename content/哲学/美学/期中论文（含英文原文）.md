Needham provides the best interpretation of the concept of "ze" from the texts of Wang Bi, as mentioned in his book "Science and Civilisation in China," Volume 2, Chapter 2, 18f. Needham's analysis of "ze" can be summarized as follows:
1. "Ze" can be understood as "therefore" or "thus." It signifies a logical or causal connection.
2. It is associated with principles and regulations, such as "norms" or "rules," that govern certain actions or behaviors. For example, "changze" (常则) , and "shuize" (税则).
Moreover, Needham quotes a passage from Wang Bi to support his interpretation:
><统说观之为道，不以刑制使物，而以观感化物者也。神则无形者也；不见天之使四时、而四时不忒；不见圣人使百姓，而百姓自服也。>

Then the following distinction is made, one belongs to Whitehead-organic philosophy-Chinese thought'feature, the other belongs to the Newtonian sense of the law and the natural law.
Indeed, Needham pointed out that there was no concept of natural law in China, and the most prominent one was quoting Wang Bi's text.

We have to go further：

2. Why don't we take a closer look at Wang Bi's commentary on Laozi?
Regarding this question, we will turn to Needham's "Science and Civilisation in China," Volume 2, Chapter 13f, and the ontology section of "The Craft of a Chinese Commentator." In Chapter 13f, Needham asserts the following,
1. Chinese association-coordination thinking is not a Law of Participation but organism, which is embodied in Dong Zhongshu's article<今平地注水，去燥就湿；均薪施火，去湿就燥。百物其去所与异，而从其所与同。故气同则会，声比则应，其验噉然也。>
2.  Before the 17th century, there existed similar theories in both Western and Chinese traditions that held the idea of resonance or mutual stimulation among similar things. Although this notion is particularly unique to Chinese thought, it is not entirely absent in ancient Greek philosophy. For instance, Aristotle's three types of changes can serve as examples: spatial motion is explained by asserting that like attracts like; growth is explained by asserting that like nourishes like; and changes in qualities are explained by asserting that like influences like.

In his work, Needham distinguishes between a Law of Participation and an organism. In Needham's view of the dao, as he quotes from the "Huainanzi" text, <天道玄默，无容无则。大不可极，深不可测。> Therefore, Needham claims that everything harmoniously coexists under the dao, and the universe and all things within it follow their inherent nature and achieve spontaneous cooperation.  He also distinguishes this harmonious cosmology from something like "witchcraft," which has no connection. It is merely a coincidence where one thing participates in the anthor. For example, when someone dies in a city and a strange large boat appears by the river, these two events are unrelated but are connected as if a painter crudely combines two different scenes into one frame, claiming an inherent mysterious connection between them.

Therefore, in response to question 1, it seems we can say that in Needham's text, "dao" is not a transcendental existence, nor is it a natural of law with causal connections and necessary outcomes. Additionally, we cannot assume that any two unrelated things can establish a relationship. Instead, objects are governed by the dao and connect and runs spontaneously according to their inherent nature.
